How to Choose a Dissertation Topic: A Practical Framework

Selecting the right dissertation topic is the most critical decision of your degree. Learn how to identify viable research gaps, evaluate feasibility, and formulate defensible research questions.

Your undergraduate or master's dissertation is likely the longest, most intellectually demanding piece of independent academic work you will produce during your degree. You will spend hundreds of hours researching, analyzing, and writing about this subject. A poorly chosen topic—one that is too broad, impossible to access data for, or conceptually obsolete—will turn your project into an exhausting uphill battle.

Conversely, a well-chosen topic that targets a clear gap in published literature, aligns with available methodology, and genuinely interests you will make writing exciting and maximize your chance of achieving a Distinction. In this guide, we provide a structured framework for topic selection and refinement.

1. The 4-Stage Topic Selection Funnel

Never pick a dissertation topic in a single sitting. Move methodically through this four-tier filtration process:

Tier 1: Disciplinary Passion & Coursework Strengths Review your module marks and reading lists from past years. Which topics did you score highest in? Which seminars provoked genuine curiosity? Working on an area you enjoy sustains your motivation across 6–9 months.
Tier 2: Recent Literature Review (The Gap Identification) Examine recent peer-reviewed articles (published within the last 24–36 months) in leading journals in your discipline. Read their final sections: 'Limitations and Directions for Future Research.' Authors explicitly state which questions remain unanswered!
Tier 3: Feasibility & Access Audit Do you have realistic access to the required data, participants, laboratory equipment, or proprietary financial databases within your university's submission window?
Tier 4: Supervisor Alignment Review the published research interests of faculty members. Aligning your topic with a potential supervisor's research expertise dramatically improves the quality of mentorship you receive.

2. The Dissertation Feasibility Audit Matrix

Before confirming your topic with your department, test it against this critical feasibility matrix:

Feasibility Factor Green Flag (✓) Red Flag (✗)
Data Accessibility Public datasets (World Bank, Kaggle, SEC EDGAR), accessible student survey pools, or open-access archives. Requiring confidential interviews with C-suite executives or proprietary military/commercial records.
Scope & Scale Focused inquiry evaluating 2–3 variables across a clearly bounded industry, demographic, or timeframe. Grandiose topics: 'The Impact of Global Trade on World Poverty.' Too broad to cover rigorously.
Ethical Approval Low-risk secondary data analysis or standard anonymized adult surveys. Working with vulnerable populations, clinical trials, or sensitive data requiring complex institutional review.
Methodological Skill Matches statistical packages (R, Python, SPSS) or qualitative coding software (NVivo) you already know. Requiring advanced econometric modeling you have never been trained to execute.

3. Crafting SMART Research Questions

A vague topic title cannot guide your research; you must convert it into 1 primary research question and 2–3 secondary sub-questions:

Vague Topic: Remote work in technology companies.

Refined Research Question: 'To what extent does asynchronous communication moderate employee burnout among software developers working remotely in UK fintech scale-ups?'

Notice how the refined question specifies the independent variable (asynchronous communication), dependent variable (employee burnout), population (software developers), and industrial/geographic boundary (UK fintech scale-ups).
